How to balance a chemical equation

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of every element on both sides, because atoms are never created or destroyed in a reaction (law of conservation of mass). You balance it by changing the coefficients (the big numbers in front of formulas) and never the small subscripts.

  1. Write the unbalanced equation with correct formulas.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
  3. Start with the element that appears in the fewest compounds, and leave hydrogen and oxygen for last.
  4. Adjust coefficients until every element matches. Use the smallest whole numbers.
  5. Count again to check.

Worked example: burning propane

Unbalanced: C3H8 + O2 → CO2 + H2O. Carbon: 3 on the left, so put 3 in front of CO2. Hydrogen: 8 on the left, so put 4 in front of H2O. Oxygen on the right is now 3 × 2 + 4 = 10, so put 5 in front of O2.

C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O

Ionic equations and charges

For ions, type the charge after a ^ sign: Fe^3+, SO4^2-, Cl^-, H^+. Electrons are written e^-. Calvo then balances the atoms and the total charge together. For example, MnO4^- + Fe^2+ + H^+ = Mn^2+ + Fe^3+ + H2O gives 1, 5, 8, 1, 5, 4, and the charge row in the check table shows +17 on both sides. Pasted superscripts such as Fe³⁺ also work. Always type Fe^3+, not Fe3+, because a plain 3 would be read as a subscript.

How Calvo does it

Calvo gives every formula a letter, writes one atom-balance equation for every element (and one for the total charge when ions are present), solves them exactly with fractions, then scales to the smallest whole numbers and checks every element again before showing the answer. The step-by-step panel under the result shows exactly this working. In class you may use hit-and-trial instead; the final answer is the same. It accepts brackets such as Al2(SO4)3, hydrates such as CuSO4·5H2O (typed CuSO4.5H2O), and ignores state symbols like (s), (l), (g), (aq).

What this balancer cannot do

It cannot tell you whether a reaction really happens. In redox reactions it does not add missing H2O, H+ or OH− for you: type them in the equation and Calvo balances what you typed. If an equation can be balanced in more than one way, Calvo says so instead of guessing.

FAQ

How do I balance a chemical equation?

Count the atoms of each element on both sides and change the coefficients (not the subscripts) until all counts match, using the smallest whole numbers. Or type the equation into the balancer above.

Can I use the arrow or equals sign?

Yes. Use = or -> between the reactants and products, and + between compounds. Example: Fe + O2 = Fe2O3.

Does it support brackets and hydrates?

Yes. Brackets like Ca(OH)2 and Al2(SO4)3 work, and hydrates can be typed as CuSO4.5H2O.

Why does it say an element appears on only one side?

Every element must appear on both sides of a balanced equation. Check the formulas for a typing mistake, for example Co (cobalt) typed instead of CO (carbon monoxide).

Why does it say there is more than one way to balance?

Some equations are really two reactions written together, so many coefficient sets work. Split them into separate reactions and balance each.

Is balancing by coefficients or subscripts?

Only by coefficients. Changing a subscript changes the substance, for example H2O becomes H2O2.

Can it balance ionic equations with charges like Fe^3+?

Yes. Write the charge after ^, for example Fe^3+, SO4^2- or Cl^-, and electrons as e^-. Calvo balances the atoms and the total charge together and shows a Charge row in the check table.

Does it show the steps? Is there an Urdu explanation?

Yes. Every answer comes with a five-step solution, and you can switch it between English and Urdu with the buttons above the balancer. The page also has solved examples for 9th, 10th, 11th and 12th class.

How do I type the arrow, ^ and electrons on my phone?

Use the Insert buttons under the input box. They add the arrow, plus, ^, minus, brackets and e^- at the cursor.
