The formulas

Attendance % = (classes attended ÷ classes held) × 100.

If you are below the required percentage, the classes you must attend in a row are ceil((T × held − 100 × attended) ÷ (100 − T)), where T is the required percentage. If you are above it, the classes you can still miss in a row are floor((100 × attended − T × held) ÷ T).

Worked example

You attended 30 of 40 classes. That is 75%. With 75% required you can miss 0 more classes in a row. If you attended 28 of 40 (70%), you must attend 8 classes in a row: (75 × 40 − 100 × 28) ÷ 25 = 8, and 36 of 48 = 75%.

Leaves left in the semester

If the semester has S classes in total, you need ceil(T × S ÷ 100) attended classes by the end. The leaves you can still take are the remaining classes minus the ones you still need to attend.

Note

Your institute may count medical leave, rounding or lab hours differently. Use this as a planning tool and follow your own policy.

Attendance rules, leaves and how to stay above the limit

Attendance policy is one of the quickest ways to lose a semester through no fault of your marks. A student who scores well in every paper can still be barred from the final exam for falling below the required percentage. This guide explains how attendance is normally counted in Pakistani colleges and universities, how to plan your leaves, and how to use the calculator to avoid being short.

What the usual rule looks like

Many Pakistani universities and colleges require at least 75% attendance in each course to appear in the final exam, and some programmes, particularly medical and technical ones, set 80% or higher. Boards and colleges have their own policies, and the exact limit is stated in the student handbook. The rule applies per course, so a student may be fine in four subjects and short in the fifth.

How the percentage is counted

The basic formula is attendance percentage = classes attended ÷ classes held × 100. Two details cause confusion:

  • Held, not scheduled. If a class was cancelled by the teacher, it usually does not count in classes held. Count only sessions that actually took place.
  • Contact hours. A 3-credit course in a typical 16-week semester meets for about 48 contact hours, so each missed hour is roughly 2% of the semester. Lab courses count their own hours.

Worked example 1: how many classes can you miss?

You have attended 17 of 20 classes, which is 85%, and the limit is 75%. Using floor((100 × 17 − 75 × 20) ÷ 75) = floor(200 ÷ 75) = 2, you can miss 2 more classes in a row before dropping below 75%. If you miss a third, attendance becomes 17 of 23, which is 73.9%.

Worked example 2: how many classes must you attend to recover?

You have attended 16 of 24 classes, which is 66.7%. To reach 75% you must attend ceil((75 × 24 − 100 × 16) ÷ 25) = 8 classes in a row. After those, you will have attended 24 of 32, exactly 75%. If the semester has fewer than 8 classes left, recovery is mathematically impossible, and the calculator warns you.

Worked example 3: planning leaves for the whole semester

A course has 48 sessions in total. To finish with 75% you must attend at least ceil(0.75 × 48) = 36 of them, so you have 12 leaves in the entire semester. At 80% you need 39, leaving only 9. Now suppose you are halfway through: 30 sessions have been held, you attended 21 (70%), and 18 remain. You need 36 in total, so you must attend 15 of the remaining 18 and can miss only 3. This is the number that matters, and the "Total classes in semester" box above gives it directly.

Why the first weeks matter

Attendance is easiest to protect early. In a 48-session course, missing 6 classes in the first month leaves you with 6 spare leaves for the remaining 34 sessions, which is enough for illness or family events. Missing 10 in the same period leaves only 2, and one bad week can end your eligibility. Treat early leaves as expensive, not free.

Keep a reserve for real emergencies

  • Do not spend all your leaves on convenience. Keep at least 3 to 4 in reserve for illness or a family emergency.
  • Ask whether medical leave with a valid certificate is excused, and by when you must submit it. Some universities allow a small percentage as medical relief.
  • Check how late arrivals are treated. Some departments mark a student absent if more than 10 or 15 minutes late.
  • Keep a screenshot or note of your own count. Disputes are easier to resolve if you can compare your record with the teacher's sheet.

Common mistakes

  • Using scheduled classes instead of classes held. This makes your percentage look worse than it is, or better, depending on the case.
  • Mixing courses. Enter each course separately. Do not combine theory and lab hours unless your university does.
  • Forgetting rounding rules. Some institutes round 74.5% up to 75%, others do not.
  • Assuming the target is "about" 75%. Most policies say at least 75%, so 74.9% fails.
  • Trusting a verbal promise. If a teacher says "do not worry about attendance", check whether the department actually agrees before relying on it.

What to do if you are already short

If the calculator shows you cannot recover, act immediately. Meet the course teacher and the department coordinator, bring any medical or official documents, and ask whether a condonation or special permission is possible. Many universities have a formal process, often with a fee or a deadline before the date sheet is issued. Waiting until the exam week usually leaves no options.

The calculator only works on numbers you enter, and nothing is saved or sent anywhere. Use it as a planning tool, and follow the attendance policy written in your own handbook.

Attendance in medical, engineering and board-affiliated colleges

Rules are not the same everywhere. Intermediate colleges follow the board's policy, which often links the roll-number slip to a minimum attendance. University programmes set the limit in their academic regulations, and professional councils may add their own requirement for clinical or lab work. If you are in a programme with hospital rotations, workshops or lab sessions, ask how those hours are counted, because missing a single lab can cost more than missing a lecture. The calculator accepts any required percentage, so you can type 80, 85 or whatever your department states.

A simple weekly routine

  1. At the end of every week, enter classes held and attended for each course. It takes under a minute per course.
  2. Write the "classes you can still miss" number next to the course in your planner.
  3. If any course shows 2 or fewer spare classes, treat it as protected and plan appointments around it.
  4. Re-check after any week with a holiday or a cancelled class, since that changes the classes-held count.

Students who do this rarely get a surprise at the end of the semester, and they can say yes to a family function or a trip without worrying, because they know exactly how much room they have.

FAQ

How do I calculate attendance percentage?

Divide the classes you attended by the classes held and multiply by 100.
