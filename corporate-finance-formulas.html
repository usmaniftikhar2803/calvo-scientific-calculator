About these Corporate Finance formulas

Corporate finance asks three questions: which projects should the firm invest in, how should it raise the money, and how much should it return to shareholders. The formulas below follow that order, starting with the cost of each source of capital, then leverage and capital structure, then investment appraisal and valuation.

Basic time-value formulas (PV, FV, annuities) and the standard financial ratios are on their own pages, linked below, so they are not repeated here.

How to use these formulas

  • Compute the cost of each source of capital first (equity, debt, preferred), then weight them to get WACC.
  • Use WACC (or a risk-adjusted rate) as the discount rate when you calculate NPV. Accept projects with a positive NPV.
  • Use market values for the weights when the question gives them. Use book values only if market values are not available.

Worked example

Question. A firm is financed with Rs. 600 million of equity and Rs. 400 million of debt. The risk-free rate is 6%, the market return is 11%, equity beta is 1.2, the pre-tax cost of debt is 8% and the tax rate is 30%.

Cost of equity (CAPM) = 6 + 1.2 × (11 − 6) = 12.0%

After-tax cost of debt = 8 × (1 − 0.30) = 5.6%

WACC = (600 × 12.0 + 400 × 5.6) / 1,000 = 9.44%

A project costs Rs. 100 million and returns Rs. 40, 50 and 60 million over three years. Discounting at 9.44%: NPV = Rs. 24.07 million, so the project adds value.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the tax shield: only the cost of debt is multiplied by (1 − T), not the cost of equity.
  • Using the coupon rate as the cost of debt when the question gives the current market yield.
  • Comparing projects by payback period alone. Payback ignores cash flows after the cut-off and the time value of money.

FAQ

What is the difference between WACC and the cost of equity?

The cost of equity is the return shareholders require. WACC blends the cost of equity, debt and preferred capital according to how much of each the firm uses, and is the usual discount rate for average-risk projects.

When is NPV better than the payback period?

Almost always. NPV uses every cash flow and discounts them, so it shows the value a project adds. Payback only tells you how fast the investment is recovered.

What does the Hamada equation do?

It adjusts a beta for leverage. You unlever an observed beta to get the business risk alone, then relever it at the target debt-to-equity ratio and tax rate.
